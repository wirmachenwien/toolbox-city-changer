1. Why this toolbox?

The "Toolbox for City Changers" is for people who want to make their neighbourhood safer, greener, more inclusive and more resilient to climate change, and need practical tools to do so. It brings together experiences from Vienna, Berlin and Ljubljana [from x, y and z, each contributing this and that].

This handbook is for everyone asking:

How can we make our city more liveable?

All following chapters help answer this question step by step.

What this handbook offers

As an essential part of the Toolbox for City Changers, this handbook summarises...

  • How superblocks work as a planning concept to make your neighbourhood safer, greener and more people-friendly (chapter x)
  • How actions and interventions can make ideas for a better city visible (chapter x)
  • How street design tools can help neighbourhoods generate ideas for better design
  • How tactical urbanism can turn these ideas into reality with little effort and cost

  • And what it takes to inspire as many people as possible to support your cause and actively get involved.

2. Starting superblocks

Superblocks reduce through traffic and create space for spending time, walking, cycling, greenery and neighbourhood life. Barcelona made the concept internationally known; Berlin developed Kiezblocks, a version strongly driven by local initiatives; Vienna has a high-quality implementation in Supergrätzl Favoriten and is now discussing cheaper "low-traffic neighbourhoods".

What changes

A superblock is more than a single piece of street furniture or a greening project. The central lever is traffic management:

  • Modal filters, one-way systems, bollards and other measures prevent through traffic.
  • Access and delivery traffic remain possible, but move more slowly and under more direct control.
  • Space inside the area becomes available for spending time, routes to school, small open spaces, trees, seating and safe junctions.

Learning from Berlin

Changing Cities shows that superblock initiatives do not need perfect plans from the start. What matters is making a local group visible and keeping it able to act over time. Berlin's Kiezblock initiatives work with guides, advice, traffic counts, conversations with businesses, fact checks and ideas for actions.

The lesson for Vienna: do not wait for the finished master plan. Define a clear area, an understandable problem and your first allies.

Getting started in seven steps

  1. Define the area: Choose a neighbourhood where you can clearly identify through traffic, routes to school, heat, missing trees or unsafe junctions.
  2. Document the problem: Record observations, photos, points of conflict and, where possible, traffic counts.
  3. Form a core team: Clarify roles for research, neighbourhood contact, social media, district contacts and actions.
  4. State the goal simply: For example, "no through traffic around the school" or "safe routes and more space to spend time inside the neighbourhood".
  5. Make a first sketch: Work with a base map and consider where modal filters, one-way streets or places to spend time might fit.
  6. Build alliances: Talk to residents, parents, schools, local associations, shops and district politicians.
  7. Make your demand public: Use actions, media work, petitions, district motions or open letters, depending on the political opportunity available.

Playful interventions

Playful interventions show how differently a street could be used. They can be easy to join: chalk, mobile seating, pop-up games, temporary greenery, information stalls, street picnics or visualisations using banners.

The action should do more than look nice: it should make a demand clear. What problem does it show? What solution does it anticipate? Who should act afterwards?

3. Tactical urbanism and street design

Tactical urbanism means low-cost, temporary interventions in urban space that can test changes, make them visible and accelerate political action. This is relevant in Vienna because the city government is considering tactical urbanism and "low-traffic neighbourhoods" as cheaper forms of traffic calming.

Basic principles

Tactical urbanism works particularly well when five conditions come together:

  • The measure is rooted locally and solves a specific problem.
  • It can be implemented quickly and carries little risk.
  • It demonstrates a longer-term change on a small scale.
  • It builds relationships between the neighbourhood, initiatives, public administration and politicians.
  • It generates useful experience: What works, what needs adjusting, and who uses the space?

Six tools

The Tactical Urbanism Guide and Wir machen Wien materials identify six simple groups of tools:

  • Barriers such as bollards, concrete elements or planter rings
  • Greenery and planting elements
  • Street furniture
  • Surface design using colour
  • Signs and signage
  • Events

Quality lies in the appropriate use of an object, rather than the object itself: a painted surface is only effective if it changes behaviour, helps people find their way or makes a new use plausible.

The reality in Vienna

Vienna is highly regulated. Proposed uses of public space need review and approval, particularly by the relevant municipal departments such as MA 46, responsible for traffic management and technical traffic matters. Bottom-up interventions therefore need an appropriate permit, cooperation with the district or city, or a form of action planned in accordance with the law.

Street design as a group tool

Wir machen Wien uses street design workshops to turn dissatisfaction into concrete visions of space. The modules can be used individually or together:

  • Street Design Table: A street plan is adapted using paper, scissors and pens. The group discusses uses, space requirements and alternatives.
  • Street Banner: Street widths are visibly divided to show how much space could be available for walking, cycling, greenery or spending time.
  • Tree Love Set: Mobile tree visualisations show where shade and greenery are needed.
  • Leiwander Straßen Radar: A simple survey sheet makes quality of life, safety and the need for action visible.
  • Street Shit Bingo: A walk sharpens awareness of obstacles, hazards and absurd misallocations of street space.

Running a local workshop

  1. Choose a street or junction.
  2. Gather problems: heat, noise, pavement width, crossings, parking pressure, conflicts, missing seating or green spaces.
  3. Measure the space or understand its dimensions using plans.
  4. Build alternatives: minimal, bold, long-term.
  5. Define an immediate measure: Which temporary action best demonstrates the idea?
  6. Clarify permits and alliances.
  7. Document the action afterwards: photos, people's views, uses, media coverage and political reactions.

4. Mobilisation and strategies for change

Mobilisation is more than reach. An initiative needs to understand why people participate, what story it tells about change and what assumptions underpin its strategy.

Your own narrative

A narrative of engagement answers four questions:

  • What are we working towards?
  • Why is now a good window of opportunity?
  • Who can build power with us?
  • What change should our pressure bring about?

Wir machen Wien uses the Platz für Wien campaign as an example: many signatures, strong media coverage and Vienna's 2020 election were meant to create political pressure for a different transport policy. A local initiative can start smaller, but needs the same clarity.

Theories of change

Theories of change are assumptions about how social and political change happens. They help you choose tactics deliberately instead of simply trying everything at once.

Three perspectives particularly useful for local urban initiatives are:

  • Major leaps: Change happens when a problem is redefined, new actors emerge and media attention opens a window.
  • Grassroots: People build power through collective action, even without an institutional position.
  • Group formation: Shared values, identity and norms keep a group able to act and give it a distinct character.

Elements of action

These theories suggest practical options:

  • Set the agenda
  • Gain prominent supporters
  • Generate media interest
  • Use a window of opportunity
  • Influence decision-makers
  • Forge alliances
  • Reframe issues
  • Make social norms visible
  • Build capacity
  • Communicate familiar values

Not everything suits every initiative. A small group should consistently use two or three suitable levers rather than ten half-heartedly.

Campaign structure

A simple campaign structure is enough to start:

  1. Goal: What should change, and by when?
  2. Decision point: Who can approve or prevent it?
  3. Target groups: Who do we need to activate, persuade or put under pressure?
  4. Milestones: Which visible intermediate goals demonstrate progress?
  5. Tasks: Who handles research, outreach, actions, documentation, media and political discussions?
  6. Rhythm: When does the group meet, when does it communicate publicly, and when does it evaluate?

5. Monitoring: making impact visible

Monitoring means documenting progress and impact so that an initiative can learn, communicate and make political arguments. It does not need to be complicated. What matters is deciding what to observe before an action.

What can be measured

Simple indicators work well for superblocks, tactical urbanism and street design:

  • Traffic volumes: How many cars, bicycles and pedestrians use a section?
  • Speed and conflicts: Where do people drive too fast, and where do dangerous situations arise?
  • Use for spending time: Do people stop or sit down, do children play, do conversations emerge?
  • Climate and comfort: Is there shade, urban heat, greenery, drinking water or noise reduction?
  • Support: How many people sign, attend meetings, share content or offer help?
  • Political progress: What motions, discussions, commitments or administrative reviews are taking place?

A minimal monitoring plan

  1. Document the baseline: photos, a map, a short problem note and an initial count.
  2. Define a target: for example, less through traffic, more safe crossings or more supporters.
  3. Choose a method: traffic count, walk, interview, street radar, before-and-after photos or media monitoring.
  4. Assign responsibilities: one person coordinates data, one photos, and one quotes and contacts.
  5. Present results: One page is often enough: problem, method, key figures, photos and demand.
  6. Learn: What needs adjusting in your demand, action or alliance-building?

Monitoring as mobilisation

Monitoring can itself be an action. A street traffic count gets people talking. A walk with neighbours makes problems visible together. A map of dangerous spots can attract new supporters.

6. Work plan and sources

This chapter summarises the toolbox as a work plan. It can serve as a checklist for a new or existing neighbourhood initiative.

A 30-day start-up plan

Week 1: Problem and area

  • Define the area and mark it on a map.
  • Formulate three main problems.
  • Gather photos, initial observations and existing plans.
  • Establish a core team and communication channel.

Week 2: Strategy and goal

  • Write your narrative in five sentences.
  • Decide whether to focus on superblocks, tactical urbanism, street design or mobilisation.
  • List decision-makers and potential allies.
  • Define your first demand and next milestone.

Week 3: Visibility

  • Plan a small public action.
  • Organise a walk, a count or a street design session.
  • Talk to residents, schools, shops or associations.
  • Prepare materials for social media and local media.

Week 4: Evaluation and the next point of pressure

  • Summarise results.
  • Refine your demand.
  • Contact district politicians, public administration or media strategically.
  • Set the next date and assign tasks to complete by then.

Guiding principle

Good initiatives combine four things: a clear demand, a visible local group, a plausible spatial solution and evidence that change is needed and achievable.
